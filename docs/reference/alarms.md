# Alarms

Every condition the engine raises about itself, what it means, and what to do
about it.

An alarm reaches you four ways, and they are the same table evaluated once: a
`crewlet.alarm.active{kind}` gauge your collector scrapes, a named `WARN`
line when it starts and another when it clears, carrying how long it was up,
a count on the node's health envelope (`GET /health` and the
dashboard's health push carry `alarms: {count, worst}`, where `worst`
is the alarm that has been firing longest), and the node's row on the fleet
view: each node's presence heartbeat carries every alarm firing on it with when
it was raised, and `GET /fleet` lists them per node (`alarms`), so
one read covers every node. And it wakes the agents that may read the
deployment's health: each time an alarm fires or clears on a node, or a node's
first evaluation since it started finds it firing, every agent seat whose role
carries `health_tools` is notified once and pointed at `fleet_health` for
what is firing now (see [Alarm wakes](../concepts/event-system.md#alarm-wakes)).
Nothing has to be polled for any of them: every node running a state log,
which is every data node, evaluates the whole table on its own position
heartbeat, every 10s, and again the moment
the measurements it takes every 15m (each log's daily intake, the vector
coverage, the store's connection-pool waits) land, so an alarm whose
threshold is 1m is raised within a beat of passing it.

The log line is the one to read first. It carries the measurement that raised
the alarm, in the units of the thing measured, and the remedy from the table
below.

Each kind is one alarm, however many logs it stands on. These are about a log
rather than the node:

`log_headroom`, `log_ceiling_short`, `trim_blocked`, `deferred_old`, `floor_unknown` and `census_drift`.

Each of those names every log it stands on in its `domains` list, with that
log's own measurement, and leads with the most pressing log's, so the health
count and the gauge (one series per kind, reading 1 while any log's stands)
agree about how many problems there are. Their log lines are written per log:
each log the alarm comes to stand on writes its own `alarm_raised`, and each
log it stops standing on its own `alarm_cleared`, carrying the log as
`domain`, so a second log joining an alarm that is already up, and the first
one leaving it, are each said.

| Alarm | What it means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| `apply_lag` | This node is more than a minute behind the log. Being behind does not take its copy out of service; a position that stops moving does. | Check this node's applier: `crewlet retention status` names the domain and its position. A node that is behind keeps the seats it holds and claims no new ones. Only if its position stops moving for the stall grace, or it holds a record it cannot decode past the deferral grace, is its copy wrong rather than behind: that log's copy then goes out of service, and its operations go to the other data nodes until the copy recovers; the node's seats stay. |
| `read_refusals` | Reads are being refused for something other than ordinary lag, and have been for longer than a heartbeat. | Read the refusal code in the logs. Anything other than `behind` or `too_stale` is a fault rather than a wait. |
| `barrier_slow` | The read barrier (the append every linearizable read waits on) is spending a quarter of the whole read budget. | The barrier is an append and a wait: check the broker's own latency and this node's apply drain before looking anywhere else. |
| `log_headroom` | A log is within a tenth of the byte ceiling its ordinary writes are held to. One alarm names every such log, leading with the one with the least room. A full log refuses writes rather than dropping records; on every log that claims identity (every log but the vectors' and the usage domain's) an eviction still lands in the gate reserve above that ceiling. | Raise the log's ceiling with `crewlet retention set-capacity` during a maintenance window, or find out why the trim is not advancing (`crewlet retention status` names the term holding it). A full log refuses writes; it does not drop records. On a log that claims identity the top of the ceiling is kept for gate records, so if the term is a node that is gone, `crewlet retention evict` still lands and unpins the trim. |
| `log_ceiling_short` | At the rate this log took in over the last day, its byte ceiling holds less than the `min_age` window the trim keeps, so it will fill and refuse writes with every trim term satisfied. | Raise the log's ceiling with `crewlet retention set-capacity` during a maintenance window, to at least the window's worth at this rate, or shorten `stream.tracker_retention.min_age` if the deployment does not need that window. Unblocking the trim cannot help: it never removes a record younger than min_age. |
| `backup_age` | No verified backup has been recorded, or the newest is older than the policy asks for. The trim does not advance either way, and the alarm names every log whose trim the backup term is holding past its replay window, so those logs raise no `trim_blocked` of their own. | Run `crewlet backup` against any node, whatever its roles, and check whatever was meant to run it. The trim does not advance past a backup older than the policy, and does not advance at all until there is one. |
| `trim_blocked` | A log's trim has been blocked for longer than its `min_age` replay window plus one trim tick, and the log is keeping records older than that window, so it is holding what a working trim would have removed and growing toward its ceiling. One alarm names every such log and the term holding each, leading with the one held longest; a log the backup term holds while `backup_age` fires is named there instead. A young fleet blocked on its first backup or snapshot donors does not raise it: nothing in its log is past the window yet. | The term holding each log names what to fix, and `crewlet retention status` prints it with what it has and what it wants. Until it is fixed the log grows toward its ceiling by a day of records every day. |
| `deferred_old` | This node has been holding a record it cannot apply on a log for longer than the thirty-minute deferral grace. One alarm names every log that is, leading with the one held longest. The node no longer serves its copy of each log named: what the estate routes to that log is answered by the other data nodes, and every other log's copy keeps serving here. The identity log is the one whose requests are not all routed: what this node answers from its own identity rows — the sign-ins, sessions and tokens of anybody the held record may be about, and every directory decision — has answered 503 here since the record was held, and goes on doing so until this node can read it. | This node is running a build that cannot decode records its peers are writing. Upgrade it. Its copy of each log named has already gone out of service, and what the estate routes to that log is answered by the other data nodes; every other log's copy keeps serving here. On the identity log, what this node answers from its own rows is not routed: the sign-ins, sessions and tokens of anybody the held record may be about, and every directory decision, answer 503 here until the node can read the record. |
| `floor_unknown` | This node has been unable to read a log's trim floor for four heartbeats, so every read of that log here refuses. One alarm names every such log, which is every log at once when coordination is what cannot be reached. | Read the cause. An unreachable coordination store clears when coordination does, and every log on this node says so at once. A log's row in the register that does not read as its floor is named by the trim's `retention_trim_failed` line and by every write it refuses, with the key to remove. A floor published at a later generation than this node's means that log was re-anchored and this node was not (see re-anchoring in docs/guides/retention.md). Every read of a named log on this node, and its every write at an expectation of zero, is refused until its floor can be read. A log whose floor has gone unread for less than four heartbeats is not named: its reads are still served from the floor it last read, though its writes at an expectation of zero are already refused. See docs/guides/retention.md, "An unreadable trim floor". |
| `prefetch_slow` | The knowledge scans this node runs for turns' prefetches are over their budget. Every turn whose prefetch it scans waits for the scan before its first token. | Every turn whose knowledge prefetch this node scans waits for the scan before its first token, whichever node the turn runs on. Check this node's store latency (`pool_starved`, `barrier_slow`) and the corpus against what one node's share can scan in the budget: adding a data node divides the buckets again, with no configuration and no rebuild. See docs/guides/search.md. |
| `search_slow` | Interactive search is over its target. The corpus has outgrown what one node's share of it can scan in the budget. | The corpus has outgrown what one node's share can scan in the budget. Adding a node divides the buckets again, with no configuration and no rebuild. See docs/guides/search.md. |
| `search_degraded` | Searches are being answered without their semantic half: the embeddings provider or the vector domain is failing, or a node's copy of the vectors is out of service. | The embeddings provider or the vector domain is failing, or a node's copy of the vectors is out of service (its `estate_copy_not_served` line names the fault), which the asking node answers by giving that node's range (its own included) to the others for half a minute. Search still answers; it answers less well, and silently. |
| `search_scoped` | Searches are being answered over part of the corpus because a node did not answer its bucket range, or its copy of what it scans is out of service. | A node did not cover its bucket range, so part of the corpus went unscanned: it was unreachable; or its own lexical index has not finished its first lap, which is what a node that joined a few minutes ago looks like and clears itself; or its copy of what the search reads is out of service (its `estate_copy_not_served` line names the domain and the fault), which does NOT clear itself: it lasts until that copy recovers or the node adopts a snapshot. Either reply costs only the search that hears it (the asking node gives that node's range, its own included, to the others for half a minute), so a fraction that stays up is a node that keeps answering so. The answers were complete for what was searched and silent about what was not; the log line names who was absent. |
| `history_partial` | Fleet history reads (turns, traces, the event log) are being answered without every node, because one did not answer inside the fleet read budget. | Turn-level history lives only on the node that published it, so a partial answer is missing that node's rows, and every answer names the node in its `coverage`. A node that has left the fleet is gone with its detail, and the aggregates survive it in the usage domain; a live node that keeps missing the budget is slow on its own store or its route, and its own `pool_starved` and `apply_lag` alarms say which. |
| `recall_below_floor` | Less of the corpus has current vectors than semantic recall claims to cover. | The embed duty is behind. Semantic recall is answering from a corpus it does not cover. |
| `ivf_recall_below_floor` | The latest measurement of the semantic index found recall against the exact scan below the floor, in a query shape a search is issued in, even probing every list, which is the full scan's own candidate pool, so the first stage is below the floor on this corpus with or without the index. | The 1-bit first stage is failing this corpus, index or not: `crewlet search eval` against a backup's copy of the estate measures the full scan beside the index in every query shape and will say the same. The remedy is the evaluation's: raise BinaryOversample, then an int8 first stage, both code changes (see docs/guides/search.md). No index is installed meanwhile, so searches answer from the full scan at the recall the evaluation reports. |
| `records_gated` | An apply gate dropped a record. A gated record is recoverable by nothing. | A gated record is recoverable by nothing. Each drop's `statelog_record_gated` log line names the `gate` that dropped it, the record's `position` and `kind`, and the `writer` (the node that wrote it); this is worth reading today. |
| `feed_unreadable` | A change record no build on this node can read. It redelivers for ever, so every wake behind it is waiting too. | A record no build on this node can read. It redelivers for ever rather than being dropped, so the wakes behind it are waiting too. Upgrade the node past it, or the feed stops moving. |
| `maintenance_open` | A maintenance operation has been open for an hour. Maintenance stops every publisher on every node. | Maintenance stops every publisher on every node. Finish it or abandon it; nothing is being written while it is open. |
| `volume_low` | A volume holding this node's databases has less free space than the next restore, vacuum or snapshot needs for a second copy and for the write-ahead log a copy holds back there while it runs, or could not be measured at all. The alarm names the volume. | Add space to the volume the alarm names. Where it could not be measured, fix what stops it being read. A backup, a vacuum and a peer's join all need the room, and each fails partway through without it; a backup's or a snapshot's copy also needs room there for the write-ahead log it holds back while it runs, which the alarm counts. |
| `wal_large` | The write-ahead log has grown past a gibibyte, which means a checkpoint is not happening: a backup's or a snapshot's copy holds it back for the copy's run, which the alarm names, and anything else holding it is a reader holding a snapshot open. | A checkpoint is not happening past what something still reads. When the alarm names a copy — a backup's or a donor snapshot's — the copy holds the log back for its whole run, so it grows by what the node writes meanwhile, and the first checkpoint after the copy ends folds it back in and clears the alarm: make room for it, or take the copy at a quieter hour. When it names none, a reader is holding a snapshot open, and the log has no other symptom until the volume fills. |
| `pool_starved` | Callers are queuing for a database connection before their query starts. | Raise `store.max_open_conns`, or find the caller holding one. Every read on this node is queuing before it starts. |
| `census_drift` | A log is taking more than twice the linearizable reads its census allows (125 a day per agent seat, at least one seat, plus what the object store's collector reads on its own schedule, plus, on the code log whether or not the company hosts code now, the git reads its ceiling was sized for: a tenth of it in barriers over the trim's min_age), so every sizing decision under it is stale. | Re-derive the log's ceiling and the trim's cadence from the real rate. See `stream.tracker_retention` in docs/getting-started/configuration.md. A code log's git census is a share of the ceiling its stream holds, so it moves only when the log is resized: `crewlet retention set-capacity CREWLET_CODE_LOG` (docs/guides/retention.md); `stream.code_log_max_bytes` sizes only a log not yet created. |
| `objects_missing` | Some of the company's files cannot be read: the collector's last audit asked the object store for every file's bytes, and some were not there, or were there at the wrong size, under the wrong digest or as nothing the store can read, or the store failed to answer for them twice while it answered for the rest. Those files cannot be downloaded or read until they are restored, or the store serves them again. | The backend lost or changed bytes it had acknowledged, or will not serve them: check its own health (the NATS bucket OBJ_crewlet_files on the data nodes, or the S3 bucket) and restore those files from a backup, or upload them again (see docs/guides/backup.md). `crewlet objects status` names them, and what was found of each. |
| `objects_bucket_near_ceiling` | The fleet's own object bucket on the broker holds more than four fifths of the bytes it reserves. A full bucket refuses every upload, though nothing else on the broker is affected. | Once the bucket is full every upload is refused. Raise its reservation (`nats stream update OBJ_crewlet_files --max-bytes=<bytes>`, within what the broker can grant), or move the files to an S3-compatible bucket with `crewlet objects migrate --to s3` (see docs/concepts/object-store.md). |
| `objective_burn` | A service objective this node measures (publishing, serving a document, a frame becoming ready in a viewer's browser, a datastore verdict) is failing fast enough to spend its month's error budget early: in both the long and the short window of one of the two burn-rate pairs. | Find the failing events where the objective is measured (docs/reference/service-objectives.md names each one's source and its own instrument), starting from `crewlet.objectives.events` by `objective` and `outcome`. A fast burn spends a fiftieth of the month's error budget every hour and needs attention now; a slow burn spends a twentieth every six hours. The alarm clears within minutes of the fault ending, once the short window is healthy again. |
| `clock_skew` | A clock stands further from the fleet's than the hour the object store's deletions assume: this node's own, or the clock of the broker member leading the coordination store's position register, and the alarm says which. The fleet's clock is the median of the data nodes' clocks, measured on this node's position heartbeat off the register: each node's row carries the instant the node stamped on it and the instant the broker stored it. A node whose own clock moved raises it about itself; a member whose clock moved is raised about by every node. The object collector's grace and the code host's retired-pack sweep each judge an instant one machine stamped by another machine's clock, and both hold only while every node and that member keep within that hour of the fleet's clock. | Run an NTP client (chrony, systemd-timesyncd) on the machine whose clock the alarm names. Where it names this node's clock, that is this node. Where it names the clock of the broker member leading the position register, every node raises it alike and the clock to fix is that member's alone: on an embedded broker one of the data nodes — the one also raising this alarm about its own clock — and on an external NATS cluster the server leading the register's stream, which `nats stream info KV_crewlet_statelog_positions` names. With two data nodes their median is the midpoint, which neither clock outvotes: both are named once they stand more than twice the bound apart, and either may be the one that moved. Two deletions rest on every node and that member keeping within the bound of the fleet's clock, and so on any two machines keeping within twice it: the object collector deletes an object once its key is a day old by the collector's clock, while a write may still name a key twelve hours old by the deciding node's; and the code host's code-retention duty sweeps a retired pack's row once the broker's instant on its retirement is a day old by the duty holder's clock, while a clone that began on the pack may still be reading it for most of that day. A clock past the bound can delete an object a write or a read still needs. The measure is the coordination store's position register, read on every heartbeat: each data node's row carries the instant the node stamped on it and the instant the broker stored it, so each says how far that node's clock stands from the member's, and the median of them is the fleet's clock, off by no more than a heartbeat's own work between the two instants. See docs/guides/deployment.md. |
| `code_maintenance_refused` | This node's git cache cannot write the bitmap of some repositories, because a bitmap over each (about 600 bytes an object, with room for a clone of the repository beside it) needs more memory than the node can give its background work: its memory, or its cgroup's limit, less what it keeps for its git lanes, the store, the broker and the engine. Those repositories are indexed without a bitmap and served more slowly: a clone walks their history. A repository this node maintains whose next published layer it cannot write counts too — its layer's bitmap is 600 bytes an object of the repository — since no git node then publishes it, and every git node serves the packs pushed since its last publication outside every bitmap. | Each repository is still served and still indexed: the cache writes its multi-pack-index without the bitmap, so lookups stay fast, but a clone counts objects by walking the history rather than reading the bitmap: slower, never wrong. A bitmap is charged at 600 bytes an object of the repository, and 240 more for room beside it for a clone of the repository; each `code_cache_maintenance_refused` line names a repository, the step refused and its charge (a step `index` or `graph` refused as well is a repository served from its packs' own indexes; a step `publish` is a repository this node maintains whose next published layer it cannot write, at 600 bytes an object of the repository, which falls to this node because no git node advertises a budget that holds it — give one git node that memory, and it becomes the repository's maintainer: docs/guides/deployment.md, Sizing git nodes). The budget is the node's memory (its cgroup's limit where one is set, read again every ten seconds) less what it keeps for its git lanes, the store, the broker and the engine, on the `code_cache_opened` line. Give the node more memory; a container resized in place is held to its new limit within ten seconds, without a restart. Or, where git.max_transfers is set, give it fewer transfers. What the node keeps is fixed when it boots: 224 MiB for every transfer its git lane admits, whatever it serves, and 896 MiB for each core its memory backs at 2 GiB a core. An unset git.max_transfers is sized to the memory, no more than one for every 512 MiB, and leaves the background work 256 MiB for each backed core; a figure set by hand is kept as written, and leaves it nothing once its transfers' 224 MiB each reach the memory less the cores' 896 MiB each: at as little as 56% of the transfers the memory holds at 224 MiB each, where the node has a core for every 2 GiB of it or more. A budget of zero clears when git.max_transfers is lowered or unset, or when the node is given memory past what it keeps, in place or at a boot. A restart alone keeps the same lanes over the same memory, and clears it only where git.max_transfers is unset and the memory was lowered after the node booted, since the lanes are then sized to what it has. See docs/concepts/code-host.md. |
| `code_compaction_behind` | Repositories this node maintains — the git node that merges their packs and publishes their layers, the first in each repository's order whose background budget holds its publication — hold the most live packs a repository may, so every push carrying objects into them is refused `compaction_behind` until a compaction of theirs lands. Raised by their maintainer alone, from the listing it polls every minute, for as long as any repository it maintains stays at the bound. | Every push carrying objects adds a pack, and the repository's maintainer merges them on a minute's poll; pushes that carry nothing — deletions, refs moved onto commits the repository holds — still land meanwhile. Read this node's log for the repository. `code_cache_compaction_refused` says the build's memory charge, on the line beside the budget, is past what this node can give its background work: give it more memory, or fewer git transfers, as for a refused bitmap. `code_compaction_refused` names a committer's refusal, a repository deleted, or an object store with no room (`store_full`: make the room the line names, and the compaction is tried again once the store has it). `code_compaction_failed` names the build, an upload or the committer's answer that failed, each tried again at the next poll. With none of those and the count still standing, the repository takes pushes faster than one node merges them: `crewlet.code.compact.duration` beside the pushes' rate says by how much. | `code_commit_busy` | This node's committer — the data node a repository's pushes are decided on — refused proposals `busy` within the last trim tick because it could not hold them or reach them in time: a repository taking pushes faster than one committer commits them, its queue full or its proposals waiting in it until their asker could wait no longer, or this node committing for too many busy repositories at once, its whole bound held. Each was decided by nobody and is sent again. Raised by the committer's node alone, whichever node received the pushes, and only for a backlog: a proposal that arrived with its asker's attempt already spent, or one that lost every compare-and-set round, is counted and logged and raises nothing. | Nothing of a refused proposal landed, and nothing is lost: a git client runs the push again, a compaction is planned again at its node's next pass and a change's write is sent again, each paying another round trip, and a person meeting `busy` pushes again themselves. A repository's queue fills when it takes pushes faster than one committer commits them (docs/concepts/scaling.md measures the rate), so spread the pushes over repositories: each has a queue and a chain of records of its own, and repositories rank different data nodes first. A proposal that waited in the queue until its asker could wait no longer is the same backlog drained too slowly for the queue to fill: the committer's batches take longer than the pushes keep arriving — the code log slow to append (`crewlet.statelog.publish.duration` on the `code` domain), or a repository's protection rules costly to decide — so the same remedies hold, and `crewlet.code.commit.batch.size` beside the busy counter says how full the batches ran. The node's whole bound is one node committing for too many busy repositories at once: each repository's committer is the first live data node in its rendezvous order, so more data nodes spread them. A seat pushing in a loop is held to `code.limits.pushes_per_seat_day` a day. See docs/concepts/code-host.md. |
| `code_checks_backlog` | Queued attempts of checks have waited for a runner longer than their own timeout — each judged at the timeout_seconds of the definition it was queued under, the time its author gave the check to run — so a change whose head they check waits on them past anything its checks file planned for. An alarm that fires on a healthy node is a defect in this table, not a
threshold for an operator to tune: each one fires at the number that already
decides something: the grace that takes a copy out of service, the grace
that stops a node serving a copy it cannot decode, the budget a caller was
promised, the replay window a log's ceiling was sized to hold.

A seat whose token window is spent is NOT an alarm, because nothing is wrong
with the node: the ceiling is doing what it was set to do. Its mail is parked
on its inbox until the window turns over or a revision raises the ceiling.
The `seat_budget_parked` line names the window and when it resets, and
the budgets surfaces show the refusing window. See the budget park in
docs/concepts/agent-runtime.md.
